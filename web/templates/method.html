How this works

Every light has one fixed rule, written down before scoring and applied identically to every crash. The thresholds are common rules of thumb; they were not adjusted to make crashes light up.

Scoring a crash

Warnings are counted against the market peak (the S&P 500's highest close before the fall), not the official recession start, because investors care about the market.

Warned (months early)

Switched on in the 24 months before the peak, and was still on at some point in the final 12 months.

Long on

On in the final 12 months, but switched on more than 24 months before: a long-running condition rather than a fresh warning.

Late

First switched on only after the peak, before the market bottomed.

Did not fire

Not on before or during the fall.

  • Back-to-back crashes: when a crash follows soon after another, lights that switched on within 12 months of the previous bottom are not counted as warnings.
  • All data is monthly. Quarterly figures (GDP, debt, profits) are dated at the end of their quarter.

Limitations

  • We use today's revised data, not the figures people actually saw at the time.
  • Different thresholds would give different lead times.
  • Seven crashes is a small sample. The hit rates are illustrations, not statistical proof.
  • The Shiller CAPE data has not been updated since 2023, so that light is unavailable on the Lights today page.
  • 2020 was an outside shock; lights that were on then reflect a softening economy, not a forecast of a pandemic.
